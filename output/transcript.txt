00:00:01  15.1 · Building a double integral / question
Suppose this curved roof were made of glass. How much space is underneath it? A formula might tell you the answer. But first, let us build an answer you can see.

00:00:11  15.1 · Building a double integral / tile
Choose one small square on the floor. Lift it until it touches the roof. You have made a column. Its height comes from the function. Its base comes from the little square.

00:00:21  15.1 · Building a double integral / product
So this one piece contributes height times base area. The height changes from place to place. That is why counting the squares alone cannot give us the volume.

00:00:31  15.1 · Building a double integral / assemble
Now do that everywhere. The columns leave a staircase along the roof. Add their volumes, and you get an estimate. Each term in this sum belongs to one particular column.

00:00:42  15.1 · Building a double integral / refine
Make the squares smaller. The staircase begins to disappear. Nothing fundamentally new is happening: we are still multiplying a height by an area, and adding the pieces.

00:00:53  15.1 · Building a double integral / integral
The double integral names the number these estimates approach. The double sign means our sample points spread across a two dimensional floor. The answer is a volume because we chose height as the integrand.

00:01:08  15.1 · Fubini: slices of a solid / row
There is a clever way to organize all those columns. Keep x fixed, and collect a single row. Together, the little columns approximate a vertical slice through the solid.

00:01:19  15.1 · Fubini: slices of a solid / inner
Adding along y gives the area of that slice. During this first integration, x is frozen. The slice area still depends on which x you chose.

00:01:28  15.1 · Fubini: slices of a solid / outer
Now slide the slice across the floor. Add its area times its tiny thickness. The outer integral adds the slices, and the result is the original volume.

00:01:39  15.1 · Fubini: slices of a solid / switch
We could slice in the other direction. For a continuous function on a rectangle, Fubini says both groupings give the same number. We have changed the bookkeeping, while keeping all the same pieces.

00:01:51  15.1 · Fubini: slices of a solid / example
For height x plus y, over this two by one rectangle, the inner integral is x plus one half. Integrating from zero to two gives three. Read the differentials from the inside outward: first y, then x.

00:02:08  15.2 · Reading bounds from a picture / curves
But a floor need not be a rectangle. Here is the region trapped between y equals x squared and y equals x. How do we tell the integral which points belong inside?

00:02:19  15.2 · Reading bounds from a picture / vertical
Draw one vertical scan line. It enters at the parabola and leaves at the straight line. As x travels from zero to one, this little strip sweeps the whole region exactly once.

00:02:31  15.2 · Reading bounds from a picture / write
That drawing gives the bounds. The lower edge becomes x squared. The upper edge becomes x. The outer bounds describe the journey of the scan line. No guessing is required.

00:02:43  15.2 · Reading bounds from a picture / horizontal
Now turn the scan line sideways. At a fixed height y, the line is the left boundary: x equals y. The parabola is the right boundary: x equals the square root of y.

00:02:54  15.2 · Reading bounds from a picture / reverse
These two integrals describe precisely the same shaded region. Switching the order means solving for new endpoints. It does not mean swapping the differential symbols and leaving the bounds alone.

00:03:07  15.2 · Reading bounds from a picture / area
As a quick check, set the height equal to one. Then you are simply adding floor area. The vertical strip has length x minus x squared. The total area is one sixth.

00:03:19  15.3 · Why polar area contains r / grid
For a disk, rectangular strips feel like the wrong building blocks. What if we gave our grid rings and spokes? Imagine this ordinary coordinate rectangle bending into a circular region.

00:03:32  15.3 · Why polar area contains r / tile
Focus on one little tile. Its radial thickness is d r. Its other edge is a short arc. An angle d theta cuts off arc length r times d theta.

00:03:43  15.3 · Why polar area contains r / stretch
Keep the angle fixed, but push the tile outward. Its angular edge grows. Twice the radius gives twice the area, even though the coordinate increments have not changed.

00:03:54  15.3 · Why polar area contains r / factor
This is where the extra r comes from. The physical area of a tiny polar tile is r times d r times d theta. The formula records a stretch you just watched happen.

00:04:05  15.3 · Why polar area contains r / dome
Now return to a roof: one minus x squared minus y squared, above the unit disk. In polar coordinates, the height becomes one minus r squared. The disk becomes a simple rectangle of coordinate choices.

00:04:19  15.3 · Why polar area contains r / compute
Multiply height by tile area. The radial integral of r minus r cubed is one quarter. One full turn contributes two pi. The volume is pi over two. The geometry simplified both the bounds and the height.

00:04:35  15.4 · Mass, balance, and density / sheet
The little floor tile could carry something other than height. Suppose it carries mass per unit area. Here the sheet becomes denser toward the right.

00:04:44  15.4 · Mass, balance, and density / mass
Density times tile area is a little mass. Add those contributions to get the mass of the sheet. We have changed what the integrand means, while keeping the same operation.

00:04:55  15.4 · Mass, balance, and density / balance
To find the balance point, let each little mass vote for its position. Weight the x coordinate by mass, add those votes, and divide by the total mass. Heavier places pull the average toward them.

00:05:08  15.4 · Mass, balance, and density / result
On the unit square with density one plus x, the mass is three halves. The balance point is five ninths across, and one half up. It shifts right because that is where the extra mass lives.

00:05:20  15.4 · Mass, balance, and density / applications
The same picture gives other applications. Average value divides the weighted total by area. A moment of inertia weights mass by squared distance from an axis. A probability density assigns probability to each tiny tile; its total must be one.

00:05:39  15.5 · The surface and its shadow / shadow
Now imagine painting the roof itself. The area you paint is larger than the area of its shadow. A floor tile and its lifted surface patch have different sizes.

00:05:49  15.5 · The surface and its shadow / tilt
Watch the patch tilt. Its shadow stays fixed, but the patch stretches. Close enough to a point, the tangent plane tells us exactly how that stretching behaves.

00:06:00  15.5 · The surface and its shadow / edges
Moving one unit in x gives the tangent direction one, zero, f sub x. Moving one unit in y gives zero, one, f sub y. Their cross product measures the area of the parallelogram they span.

00:06:14  15.5 · The surface and its shadow / factor
Its length is the square root of one plus the two squared slopes. Multiply floor area by this local factor, then add over the floor. This computes surface area rather than the volume underneath.

00:06:28  15.5 · The surface and its shadow / example
For the plane z equals x plus y, both slopes are one. Every patch is square root of three times its shadow. Above a unit square, the surface area is therefore square root of three. A horizontal roof would have factor one.

00:06:45  15.6–15.7 · Triple and cylindrical integrals / boxes
A triple integral begins with a new kind of region. Our sample points now fill a solid. The tiny pieces are boxes, with volume d x times d y times d z.

00:06:57  15.6–15.7 · Triple and cylindrical integrals / tetra
Consider the tetrahedron in the positive octant, under x plus y plus z equals one. Choose a point in its triangular shadow. A vertical column starts on the floor and stops at the sloping face.

00:07:10  15.6–15.7 · Triple and cylindrical integrals / bounds
The roof gives z equals one minus x minus y. The shadow gives y from zero to one minus x. Finally, x travels from zero to one. Read those boundaries from the inside outward.

00:07:24  15.6–15.7 · Triple and cylindrical integrals / volume
Integrate one, and you count volume. First the z integral becomes the height of a column. Then the y integral becomes the area of a slice. Finally, the x integral gives one sixth.

00:07:36  15.6–15.7 · Triple and cylindrical integrals / cylinder
For a round solid, replace the floor grid with polar tiles. Stack them vertically. Their volume is r d r d theta d z. Cylindrical coordinates are simply polar coordinates with a height attached.

00:07:51  15.8 · Three lengths on a spherical shell / ball
A ball suggests one more change of coordinates. Describe a point by distance rho from the center, angle theta around the vertical axis, and angle phi down from the positive z axis.

00:08:03  15.8 · Three lengths on a spherical shell / block
Cut out one small block from a spherical shell. There are three lengths to understand. Its radial thickness is d rho. Its north to south edge is rho d phi.

00:08:14  15.8 · Three lengths on a spherical shell / latitude
The east to west edge follows a latitude circle. That circle has radius rho sine phi. So the third edge has length rho sine phi d theta. Near a pole, the latitude circle almost disappears.

00:08:27  15.8 · Three lengths on a spherical shell / multiply
Multiply the three short lengths. The tiny volume is rho squared sine phi times d rho d phi d theta. The squared radius accounts for larger shells. The sine accounts for shrinking latitude circles.

00:08:41  15.8 · Three lengths on a spherical shell / check
For a full ball of radius a, the radial integral gives a cubed over three. The polar integral gives two. The full turn gives two pi. Together they recover four pi a cubed over three.

00:08:55  15.9 · The Jacobian: local area scaling / warp
Rings, spokes, and spherical shells are examples of a larger idea. A coordinate change is a machine that bends a grid. A unit of coordinate area need not become a unit of physical area.

00:09:08  15.9 · The Jacobian: local area scaling / zoom
Zoom into a tiny square. A smooth map turns it into an almost parallelogram shaped patch. One edge follows the derivative in u. The other follows the derivative in v.

00:09:20  15.9 · The Jacobian: local area scaling / det
The determinant of those two edge directions gives the signed area scale. Take its absolute value to obtain physical area. This is the Jacobian: the local exchange rate between the two kinds of area.

00:09:33  15.9 · The Jacobian: local area scaling / ellipse
Stretch a unit disk by two horizontally and leave its height alone. Every area doubles. The disk becomes an ellipse, so its area is two pi. A complicated boundary has become a familiar one.

00:09:47  15.9 · The Jacobian: local area scaling / rule
In general, change the region, substitute into the function, and include the absolute Jacobian. Use a smooth map that covers the region once, apart from negligible boundaries, with a nonzero determinant in the interior.

00:10:02  15.9 · The Jacobian: local area scaling / ending
That is the thread through Stewart chapter fifteen. Draw the pieces. Measure their actual size. Work out what each piece contributes. Then add. The integral is the total; the geometry tells you what belongs inside it.
