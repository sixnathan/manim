00:00  One idea, in more dimensions

What does a double integral actually mean? And why does changing coordinates suddenly introduce a mysterious extra factor? This chapter is one idea seen from several angles. Break a region into tiny pieces. Work out what each piece contributes. Then add everything. We will build that picture, learn to read integration bounds from a drawing, and watch the polar and spherical scale factors emerge from geometry. Keep asking two questions: what are the pieces, and how big is each one?

Formula (LaTeX): \mathrm{total}=\int \mathrm{local\ contribution}


00:37  15.1  |  A landscape made of columns

Imagine a landscape whose height above the floor is f of x and y. Cover the floor with a rectangular grid. Above each little tile, build a column reaching up to the landscape. Its volume is height times base area. The blue tile tells us where we sample. The yellow column tells us what we add. At first this is only an approximation. A coarse column misses some of the changing surface above its tile.

Formula (LaTeX): \Delta V\approx f(x_i,y_j)\,\Delta x\,\Delta y


01:09  Refinement is the meaning of the integral

Now shrink the tiles. The staircase of columns follows the surface more closely. For a continuous function on this rectangle, the sums converge to a single number. That number is the double integral. Notice that the two integral signs describe a two dimensional collection of sample points. They do not mean we are squaring an ordinary integral. If the function is negative, its contribution is negative too. Only a nonnegative height gives ordinary geometric volume directly.

Formula (LaTeX): \iint_R f\,dA=\lim_{\mathrm{mesh}\to0}\sum_{i,j}f(x_i,y_j)\Delta A


01:45  15.1  |  Add a row, then add the rows

We cannot literally add infinitely many columns one by one. Instead, group them into strips. Freeze x. Within that strip, integrate along y from zero to one. Since x is fixed, its contribution is simply x times a length of one. Integrating y contributes one half. The strip therefore has cross sectional area x plus one half. Now integrate those areas as x runs from zero to two. The result is three cubic units. This is an iterated integral.

Formula (LaTeX): \int_0^2\!\int_0^1(x+y)\,dy\,dx=\int_0^2(x+\frac{1}{2})\,dx=3


02:25  Fubini: two ways of grouping the same pieces

We could group the same columns into rows running the other way. For continuous functions on a rectangle, Fubini's theorem says both orders give the same total. The inner differential tells you which direction to sweep first. Read from the inside outward. Switching the order can turn an awkward calculation into an easy one. But this is a theorem with hypotheses. Continuity is a useful sufficient condition here; do not assume every improper integral permits an arbitrary change of order.

Formula (LaTeX): \int_a^b\!\int_c^d f\,dy\,dx=\int_c^d\!\int_a^b f\,dx\,dy


03:01  15.2  |  Bounds are a moving scan line

Rectangles are friendly because every strip has the same endpoints. Real regions often curve. Look at the lens between y equals x squared and y equals x. Move a vertical scan line from left to right. Each time, it enters at the parabola and leaves at the line. That observation is the entire setup. The outer bounds are zero to one. The inner bounds are x squared to x. The bounds encode a picture, rather than a rule to memorize.

Formula (LaTeX): D=\{(x,y):0\leq x\leq1,\ x^2\leq y\leq x\}


03:37  Turn the scan line sideways

Now sweep horizontally. At a fixed height y, the left edge lies on x equals y. The right edge lies on x equals the square root of y. These are the same two curves solved for x. The total region has not changed, but the description has. With height one, either order gives the area: the integral from zero to one of x minus x squared, which is one sixth. A good check is to draw one representative strip and verify both endpoints.

Formula (LaTeX): \int_0^1\!\int_{x^2}^{x}f\,dy\,dx=\int_0^1\!\int_y^{\sqrt{y}}f\,dx\,dy


04:12  15.3  |  Why a polar tile gets wider

A circular region fights a rectangular grid. So let the grid bend into rings and spokes. One tiny tile has radial thickness d r. Its other side is a short circular arc. Arc length is radius times angle, so that side has length r d theta. Multiply the two sides and you get r d r d theta. The extra r is not an algebraic tax. Farther from the origin, equally spaced spokes enclose more physical area. The differential must account for that stretching.

Formula (LaTeX): x=r\cos\theta,\quad y=r\sin\theta,\quad dA=r\,dr\,d\theta


04:49  A dome becomes a one-line calculation

Consider the dome z equals one minus x squared minus y squared, above the unit disk. In polar coordinates its height is one minus r squared. The radius runs from zero to one and the angle makes one complete turn. Each little contribution is height times polar tile area. Integrating r minus r cubed gives one quarter, and the full turn multiplies that by two pi. The volume is pi over two. The symmetry has made both the region and the integrand simpler.

Formula (LaTeX): \int_0^{2\pi}\!\int_0^1(1-r^2)r\,dr\,d\theta=\frac{\pi}{2}


05:26  15.4  |  The integrand decides what you add

The floor tile need not carry a height. It might carry density. If delta is mass per unit area, then delta times tile area is a tiny mass. Add those masses to get the mass of a thin sheet. If f represents temperature instead, the integral sums temperature weighted by area. Divide by the total area to obtain the average temperature. The operation is the same; the meaning and units of the integrand change what the answer measures.

Formula (LaTeX): m=\iint_D\delta\,dA,\qquad f_{\mathrm{avg}}=\frac{1}{A(D)}\iint_D f\,dA


06:00  Balance is a weighted average of position

To find a balance point, weight each position by the mass sitting there. The x coordinate of the center of mass is the integral of x times density, divided by total mass. The y coordinate works the same way. Here density grows toward the right, so the balance point shifts right. On the unit square with density one plus x, the mass is three halves and the center is at five ninths, one half. Moments of inertia use squared distance instead: mass farther from an axis matters much more.

Formula (LaTeX): \bar{x}=\frac{\iint_D x\delta\,dA}{m},\quad\bar{y}=\frac{\iint_D y\delta\,dA}{m}


06:36  Probability is another kind of density

A probability density uses exactly the same machinery. A small tile carries approximately density times tile area of probability. Integrating over a region gives the chance that a random point lands inside it. The density must be nonnegative and its integral over all possible positions must be one. A density value itself can exceed one; the probability of a region cannot. Likewise, a moment of inertia integrates squared distance to an axis times mass density. The geometry tells you the pieces, and the integrand tells you what each piece contributes.

Formula (LaTeX): P((X,Y)\in D)=\iint_D p(x,y)\,dA,\qquad \iint_{\mathrm{all\ space}}p\,dA=1


07:18  15.5  |  The surface is larger than its shadow

A different question: how much material would you need to cover the landscape itself? That is surface area, rather than the volume underneath. A small patch projects to a tile on the floor, but tilting makes the surface patch larger than its shadow. Near a point, replace the surface with its tangent plane. Two tangent edges are represented by the vectors one, zero, f sub x, and zero, one, f sub y. Their cross product has length the square root of one plus f sub x squared plus f sub y squared. This is the local area multiplier.

Formula (LaTeX): dS=\sqrt{1+f_x^2+f_y^2}\,dA


07:59  A tilted plane makes the factor visible

For a concrete example, take the plane z equals x plus y above the unit square. Its slope in each coordinate direction is one. Therefore every shadow tile corresponds to a surface patch with square root of three times as much area. Integrate that constant factor over the unit square: the surface area is square root of three. If the surface were horizontal, both slopes would vanish and the multiplier would become one. This surface stretching is closely related to the Jacobian idea we will meet at the end.

Formula (LaTeX): z=x+y,\quad 0\leq x,y\leq1:\qquad A=\int_0^1\!\int_0^1\sqrt{3}\,dy\,dx=\sqrt{3}


08:35  15.6  |  Fill space with tiny boxes

For a solid region, replace floor tiles with tiny boxes. Their volume is d x times d y times d z. Integrating one counts volume. Integrating a volume density counts mass. The third integral sign means the sample points now fill a three dimensional region. To choose bounds, start with a box somewhere inside the solid. Trace the innermost direction until you hit the two bounding surfaces. Then describe where the remaining two coordinates can range.

Formula (LaTeX): \iiint_E f\,dV,\qquad dV=dx\,dy\,dz


09:12  Read a solid from the inside outward

Take the tetrahedron in the positive octant below x plus y plus z equals one. At a fixed x and y, a vertical column starts on z equals zero and stops at z equals one minus x minus y. Project the solid onto the floor. That shadow is a triangle where x and y are nonnegative and x plus y is at most one. This gives y from zero to one minus x, and x from zero to one. The volume works out to one sixth.

Formula (LaTeX): \int_0^1\!\int_0^{1-x}\!\int_0^{1-x-y}1\,dz\,dy\,dx=\frac{1}{6}


09:45  15.7  |  Cylindrical coordinates stack polar tiles

A cylinder suggests a polar grid on the floor and an ordinary vertical coordinate. Extrude each polar tile through a small height d z. Its volume is r d r d theta d z. Nothing new has happened to the stretching: it is still the widening of the spokes in the floor. For a cylinder of radius a and height h, integrating one gives pi a squared h. This is a useful check that the factor r belongs in the volume element.

Formula (LaTeX): x=r\cos\theta,\ y=r\sin\theta,\qquad dV=r\,dr\,d\theta\,dz


10:17  15.8  |  Spherical coordinates follow the shell

For a ball, even cylindrical coordinates leave a curved boundary. Spherical coordinates measure distance rho from the origin, angle theta around the vertical axis, and angle phi down from the positive z axis. This last convention matters. Phi is the polar angle, not latitude above the equator. A full ball has rho from zero to its radius, theta from zero to two pi, and phi from zero to pi. Now look closely at one small block on a spherical shell.

Formula (LaTeX): x=\rho\sin\phi\cos\theta,\ y=\rho\sin\phi\sin\theta,\ z=\rho\cos\phi


10:53  Three lengths explain the spherical factor

The block has three short edges. Its radial thickness is d rho. Moving down a meridian gives length rho d phi. Moving around the latitude circle gives length rho sine phi d theta, because that circle has radius rho sine phi. Multiply all three lengths. The result is rho squared sine phi times the three coordinate changes. Near the pole, latitude circles shrink. Near the equator they are largest. The sine factor captures exactly that geometry.

Formula (LaTeX): dV\approx d\rho\,(\rho\,d\phi)(\rho\sin\phi\,d\theta)=\rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta


11:31  A ball checks the geometry

Integrate one over a ball of radius a using that volume element. The radial integral gives a cubed over three. The polar angle integral of sine gives two. The azimuthal angle contributes two pi. Together they give four pi a cubed over three. Forgetting the sine would count too much near the poles. Forgetting rho squared would fail to account for shells growing as you move outward. The familiar volume formula is a check on our tiny block picture.

Formula (LaTeX): \int_0^{2\pi}\!\int_0^\pi\!\int_0^a\rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta=\frac{4\pi a^3}{3}


12:06  15.9  |  Every coordinate change stretches tiles

Polar coordinates are one example of a general idea. Start with a tiny rectangle in a u v parameter plane. A smooth transformation sends it to an approximately parallelogram shaped tile in the x y plane. Its two edge directions come from the partial derivatives of the transformation. Their determinant measures signed area scaling. We take the absolute value because physical area is nonnegative. This determinant is the Jacobian. It is the local conversion rate between coordinate area and physical area.

Formula (LaTeX): J=x_u y_v-x_v y_u,\qquad dA=|J|\,du\,dv


12:45  Straighten the region; pay for the stretching

For example, stretch a unit disk by a factor of two horizontally. The map x equals two u, y equals v turns the disk into an ellipse. Every tiny area doubles, so the Jacobian is two and the ellipse has area two pi. In general, transform both the region and the integrand, then include the absolute Jacobian. Use a transformation that covers the region once, apart from negligible boundaries, and is locally invertible in its interior. Otherwise you may accidentally count some points more than once.

Formula (LaTeX): x=2u,\ y=v,\quad |J|=2,\quad A(\mathrm{ellipse})=2\iint_{u^2+v^2\leq1}du\,dv=2\pi


13:23  The whole chapter, in one picture

Here is the workflow to carry into the exercises. Sketch the region first. Choose coordinates that fit its shape. Draw a representative tiny piece and identify its size. Read the bounds by sweeping that piece through the region. Then integrate from the inside outward. Check the sign, the units, and the scale of the result. Double integrals, triple integrals, and Jacobians are connected by this same picture: local contribution, times the size of a piece, added over the whole region. The symbols should now remind you of geometry.

Formula (LaTeX): \mathrm{total}=\int_{\mathrm{region}}\mathrm{density}\times\mathrm{size\ of\ a\ tiny\ piece}
